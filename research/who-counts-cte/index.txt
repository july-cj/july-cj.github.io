f:T7a9,Objective. Researchers use a range of operational definitions to identify career and technical education (CTE) students in administrative data, yet little is known about how these choices influence empirical findings. This study examines how differing identification approaches shape the observed prevalence, demographic characteristics, and academic and labor market outcomes of CTE students at community colleges. Methods. Using administrative records from the Ohio community college system for nearly 350,000 first-time community college students, we implemented multiple identification approaches based on stated preferences (e.g., declared major, career-focused educational intentions) and revealed preference definitions (e.g., number of CTE credits, share of credits in CTE fields). Results. Identification approaches markedly altered who was classified as a CTE student. Stated preference approaches identified larger groups of students who were younger, more likely to be female, and showed conventional positive earnings premia for credential completion. Revealed preference approaches yielded smaller, older cohorts with higher concentrations of CTE coursework. Within this group, the threshold rule mattered: under proportional credit thresholds, non-completing students exhibited higher observed earnings than completers five years after entry, whereas fixed credit thresholds produced more conventional positive earnings premiums for credential completion. Consequently, estimated CTE credential earnings premia varied across the approaches. Contributions. The findings demonstrate that seemingly routine operationalization decisions can substantially influence descriptive and earnings-based conclusions about CTE participation.
